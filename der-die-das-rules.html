Der die das rules: how to guess German articles

Every German noun has a gender: masculine (der), feminine (die) or neuter (das). There is no single magic rule – but these der die das rules, based on word endings and word groups, let you predict the article of many nouns correctly. Here is what to learn, with clear tables and tips.

The three genders: der, die, das

German nouns are masculine (der Tisch), feminine (die Lampe) or neuter (das Buch). The grammatical gender often has nothing to do with real-life gender: das Mädchen (the girl) is neuter because of the ending -chen. On this page and in many learning materials, the genders have colours: der = blue, die = red, das = green.

A word ending that shows the gender is the most reliable clue. The tables below say “always”, “almost always”, “mostly” or “often” – and give typical exceptions in brackets.

Male people are masculine as well: der Mann, der Vater, der Onkel. Many female forms simply add -in and switch to die: der Lehrer → die Lehrerin.

Two rules that always work

  1. Compound nouns take the gender of the last part. das Haus + die Tür = die Haustür; die Kinder + der Garten = der Kindergarten.
  2. The plural article is always die (in the nominative and accusative), no matter the gender: der Tisch → die Tische, das Kind → die Kinder. The plural endings themselves vary – see plural of nouns.

Der, die, das in the four cases

Articles change with the case – the role of the noun in the sentence. This table shows the definite article (the) and the indefinite article (a/an). kein (no, not a) and possessives like mein follow the ein pattern and have a plural: keine, meine.

Examples with der Mann:

CaseGermanEnglish
NominativeDer Mann ist nett.The man is nice.
AccusativeIch sehe den Mann.I see the man.
DativeIch helfe dem Mann.I help the man.
GenitiveDas ist das Auto des Mannes.That is the man's car.

Good news for beginners: in the accusative only the masculine changes (der → den, ein → einen). Start there with the accusative case, then learn the dative case. The four cases overview puts everything together.

Memory tips for German articles

  • Never learn a noun alone. Write and say der Tisch, die Tische – article, noun and plural as one unit.
  • Use colours. Write masculine nouns in blue, feminine in red and neuter in green on flashcards and in your notebook. Your visual memory will start to “see” the right article.
  • Make pictures. Imagine every masculine noun next to something strong, every feminine noun next to something pretty, every neuter noun next to a baby – the stranger, the better.
  • Learn the endings, not lists. Twelve feminine endings already cover thousands of words.
  • Repeat little and often. Use the vocabulary trainer and play Der-Die-Das Dash for a minute a day.

FAQ: der, die, das

Is there a rule for der, die, das?

There is no single rule that works for every noun. But many endings and word groups reliably show the gender: nouns ending in -ung, -heit, -keit or -schaft are feminine, nouns ending in -chen or -lein are neuter, and days, months and seasons are masculine. For all other nouns, learn the article together with the word.

What is the plural of der, die, das?

In the nominative and accusative plural the article is always die, whatever the gender in the singular: der Tisch – die Tische, das Kind – die Kinder. In the dative plural it is den, and the noun usually gets an extra -n: mit den Kindern.

What gender do compound nouns have?

A compound noun always takes the gender of its last part: das Haus plus die Tür gives die Haustür, and die Kinder plus der Garten gives der Kindergarten.

Why does der become den?

Articles change with the case. In the accusative, the case of the direct object, only the masculine article changes: der becomes den and ein becomes einen. Die, das and the plural die stay the same. In the dative and genitive all genders change.

How can I remember der, die, das?

Always learn a noun with its article and plural, for example der Tisch, die Tische. Use colours: blue for der, red for die and green for das. Group nouns by their endings and practise a few minutes every day, for example with the Der-Die-Das Dash game.
